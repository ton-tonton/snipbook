# Essential Skills All Product Managers Must Master

**Speaker:** Sahil Seni, Senior Product Manager at OLX Group
**Host:** Institute of Product Leadership

## 📝 Executive Summary

This session is a masterclass delivered by Sahil Seni — a seasoned PM with 9+ years of experience across OLX Group, FreeCharge, Square Yards, and Tata — on the essential hard and soft skills every product manager must develop. The talk is structured into three sections: (1) hard skills, (2) soft skills, and (3) which skills matter most at each PM career level. The speaker emphasizes that product management is a marathon, not a sprint, and that the right combination of technical acumen, customer empathy, execution discipline, and interpersonal leadership determines long-term career success.

## 📚 Detailed Summary

### Part 1: Hard Skills for Product Managers

#### 1. Problem Solving & Critical Thinking

Problem solving is arguably the single most differentiating hard skill for PMs. Sahil argues that strong PMs "fall in love with the problem, not the solution." The key principle is **first-principles thinking**: breaking a complex, unstructured problem down to its core assumptions and working up from there.

- Identify **what** the problem is and **why** it needs to be solved before jumping to solutions.
- Move deliberately from the problem space to the solution space using structured frameworks.
- **Recommended frameworks:**
  - *The CIRCLES Method* by Lewis Flynn (book: *Decode and Conquer*)
  - *Continuous Discovery Habits* by Teresa Torres — especially valuable for digital products

#### 2. Customer Empathy

A PM is the **customer advocate** inside the organization. Their role is to maintain the balance between customer experience and business viability, always giving voice to the customer.

- **Talk to customers frequently** — establish a weekly cadence.
- Apply a **360° lens**: customer experience doesn't start and end within your product. It begins before the customer touches your product and continues long after (e.g., for e-commerce: ordering → delivery → returns → support).
- Customers can be **internal or external**. Internal tools (CRM panels, operations dashboards) also have "customers" — the internal teams using them.
- **Be a power user of your own product**, especially for B2C products.
- Understand **customer behavior psychology** and how to build habit-forming products.

**Recommended books:**
- *Hooked* by Nir Eyal
- *The Design of Everyday Things* by Don Norman
- *Inspired* by Marty Cagan

#### 3. Product Craft

Product craft refers to the quality and execution of your PM deliverables: PRDs, concept notes, wireframes, prototypes, and roadmaps.

- Get hands-on with key tools:
  - **Miro** — brainstorming & collaboration
  - **Figma / Balsamiq** — wireframing
  - **InVision** — prototyping
  - **Jira** — agile project management
  - **Google Analytics / Adobe Analytics / Optimizely** — analytics & A/B testing
- Build a **product portfolio** — even if not yet employed as a PM. A shared Google Drive folder with case studies, product teardowns, wireframes, and prototypes demonstrates your craft to hiring managers.
- This skill is especially critical for **APM and entry-level PM roles** where execution is the primary expectation.

#### 4. Business Understanding

PMs don't build products in isolation. They must derive their product vision from the company vision, and their product strategy from the product vision.

- Understand how your company makes money, what its top products are, and what its long-term vision (5-year, 10-year) looks like.
- Every product initiative should have a **direct line to a selected goal or outcome** (growth, acquisition, engagement, retention, monetization).
- Talk to business stakeholders. Understand revenue, profit levers, and cost structures.
- Risk management: prioritize the problem that delivers the **maximum impact** on your selected goal.

#### 5. Risk Mitigation

Product management is fundamentally about **risk management**. Building the wrong thing is expensive — dev costs are high and time is scarce.

Sahil outlines four core risks PMs must mitigate during product discovery:

| Risk | Question |
|------|----------|
| **Value Risk** | Will the customer buy or choose to use this? |
| **Usability Risk** | Can the user figure out how to use it without instructions? |
| **Feasibility Risk** | Do we have the technical capability and resources to build it? |
| **Business Viability Risk** | Does this solution generate revenue or advance a business goal? |

A strong PM validates these risks before building — through assumption testing, hypothesis validation, MVPs, and customer feedback loops — rather than launching a full feature and discovering nobody uses it six months later.

> "It's very important for product managers to figure out **what not to build** as compared to what to build."

#### 6. T-Shaped Profile

A PM should be a **generalist with at least one or two areas of deep expertise**.

- **Depth (vertical):** Master 1–2 domains (e.g., design, analytics, fintech, growth).
- **Breadth (horizontal):** Maintain working knowledge of analytics, user research, growth marketing, AI/ML, system design, B2B vs. B2C products, platform products, SaaS models, and open APIs.
- This profile makes you adaptable and valuable across different organizations and contexts.

#### 7. Technology Understanding

PMs do **not** need to code, but they absolutely must understand how technology works.

- Understand how frontend and backend communicate.
- Learn what **APIs** are, how REST APIs work, and what the major tech stacks are (LAMP, MERN, MEAN).
- Understand basics of **system design** — how large-scale systems like Netflix (video streaming) or Amazon (e-commerce) are architecturally structured.
- A working knowledge of technology helps build stronger, more trusting relationships with engineers and QAs.

**Recommended book:** *Tech Simplified for PMs and Entrepreneurs* by Deepak Singh

#### 8. Domain Knowledge

Generalist PM skills are no longer sufficient to break into specialized industries. Niche industries now require domain-specific knowledge on top of core PM skills.

- **Fintech:** Understand UPI, tokenization, blockchain, DeFi, NFTs, smart contracts, and regulatory trends.
- **Gaming:** Understand game loops, monetization models, live ops.
- Read industry reports. Stay current with regulatory changes and disruptive technologies specific to your sector.

#### 9. Analytical Mindset & Data-Driven Decision Making

Every PM decision should be backed by data. Sahil distinguishes between being *data-informed* (using data alongside intuition) vs. blindly *data-driven*.

- Define **success metrics** before every launch.
- Identify your **North Star Metric** and the supporting sub-metrics.
- Bring data to every business case or stakeholder conversation — projections, business plans, expected impact.
- Learn **SQL** at a basic level to reduce dependency on data analysts for day-to-day queries.
- Learn **experimentation**: A/B testing frameworks (Optimizely), hypothesis formulation, and assumption testing.

#### 10. Ruthless Prioritization

Everything must be prioritized — goals, outcomes, problems, users, features, and even your MVP scope. Your initial feature list will always be 10x what you can actually ship.

- Prioritize problems that deliver the **maximum business or goal impact**, factoring in feasibility and viability.
- Focus on the right **user persona** and their most critical pain points.
- Popular prioritization frameworks:
  - **MoSCoW** (Must-have, Should-have, Could-have, Won't-have)
  - **RICE** (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort)
  - OKR-based prioritization
  - User persona-based prioritization
- Prioritization improves with experience and practice — pick a framework and commit to it.

#### 11. Think Big — 10x Impact

At large organizations (Amazon, Meta, Microsoft), products may be killed if their user base drops below one million. This is the mindset scale PMs should aim for.

- Stay aware of macro trends disrupting industries: creator economy, short-form video, embedded finance, open APIs, blockchain, AI/ML.
- Ask: does this initiative lead to a 10x improvement — in users, revenue, engagement, efficiency?
- Focus on transformative bets, not incremental tweaks.

#### 12. Execution

For APMs and early-career PMs, **getting things done is the primary KPI**.

- Be proactive about identifying and resolving dependencies.
- Align with adjacent product teams and cross-functional stakeholders before they become blockers.
- Maintain momentum — never let a sprint or project stall unnecessarily.
- Own blockers. Escalate early. Don't wait for problems to surface on their own.

#### 13. Feedback Loops & Iteration

No good product is built in one shot. Products evolve through repeated cycles of launching, gathering feedback, and iterating.

- Build feedback mechanisms directly into the product: app reviews, in-product surveys, user interviews.
- Automate where possible to get a continuous stream of signal.
- Follow the loop: **launch → test → gather feedback → iterate → repeat**.

#### 14. Writing

Business writing is increasingly critical — especially in large MNCs and global organizations.

- Amazon's culture of **written narratives** (concept notes, Press Release/FAQ documents) is a best-practice model.
- Before any meeting with stakeholders, write a concept note and share it in advance for asynchronous review.
- Good writing substitutes for long status meetings and produces more thoughtful feedback.
- Develop a writing habit outside of work — LinkedIn posts, blogs, newsletters.

### Part 2: Soft Skills for Product Managers

Soft skills are often underemphasized early in a PM career, but as Sahil makes clear: **beyond Senior PM, soft skills matter more than hard skills**. They become the differentiator at Group PM, Director, VP, and CPO levels.

#### 1. Storytelling

PMs are the **salespeople of their ideas**. Resources are always constrained. You must persuade engineers, designers, business stakeholders, and senior leadership to invest in your product.

- Storytelling is what builds buy-in, maintains motivation, and gets work done without direct authority.
- Practice by grabbing speaking opportunities outside your immediate team.
- Frame problems, solutions, and roadmaps as narratives — not just bullet lists.

#### 2. Stakeholder Management

In large organizations, product managers interact with stakeholders across every function: business, engineering, design, research, analytics, sales, finance, legal, customer support, and growth.

- Set up **1-on-1 cadences** with all key stakeholders when you join a new organization. Understand their priorities, pain points, and how their function relates to your product.
- Maintain an **Excel sheet with stakeholder mapping** — who they are, their relevance to your project, and the cadence of your updates.
- Keep stakeholders informed regularly — especially before high-visibility meetings where surprises are catastrophic.
- Use stakeholder maps to manage **escalation risk**: the worst scenario is a business stakeholder telling senior leadership they had no idea what was being built.

#### 3. Leadership

Every PM — from APM to CPO — is a leader, regardless of whether they have direct reports.

- You lead a **cross-functional squad** (designer, engineers, researchers, QA, analytics) without formal authority.
- **Lead with influence:** be open to ideas, give credit generously, and take blame when things go wrong.
- **Shield your team** from scope creep and management pressure so they can do their best work.
- Learn about your teammates' crafts (design, engineering) — it builds trust and mutual respect.

#### 4. Ownership

The PM owns everything related to their product. There is no "that's not my job."

- Be willing to do unglamorous work: copy changes, chasing status updates, manual data pulls.
- Proactively track timelines and preempt delays.
- Hold yourself accountable for shipping, not just planning.

#### 5. Ability to Say No

Feature requests come from everywhere — CEOs, CPOs, business leads, sales, engineers, support. Saying yes to everything is a fast path to chaos.

- **Listen first** — acknowledge the request and the person making it.
- Ask them to **bring data**: why is this important? What is the business impact?
- Always **put the customer interest first** when evaluating requests.
- If it's not a P0, communicate clearly that it's in the backlog and will be considered in the next prioritization cycle.
- Keep requesters updated on backlog status — silence breeds frustration and escalations.

#### 6. Communication

Clear, proactive communication is foundational — especially across distributed teams and global time zones.

- Use all available channels: email, Slack, all-hands briefings, roadmap documents.
- Communicate **API/service changes** to all consuming teams well in advance — never ship breaking changes silently.
- Update OKRs and quarterly plans with engineering, research, and design partners regularly.
- Build a culture of transparency: your stakeholders should always know where the project stands.

#### 7. Sense of Curiosity

PMs must be lifelong learners, always seeking to understand the world more deeply.

- Stay curious about adjacent product areas and engineering verticals within your company.
- Know who owns what — which PM is leading search, payments, or customer support features.
- Read broadly: industry reports, tech trends, behavioral economics, emerging regulation.
- Experiment with new products and services outside your company to broaden your UX instincts.

#### 8. Team Player

No PM builds a great product alone. The product *team* is what matters.

- A full product team includes: user researcher, designer, frontend/backend engineers, QA, analytics, and PM.
- Involve engineers **from product discovery** — not just delivery. Their early input improves feasibility and builds ownership.
- Always share the **"why"** behind the project so team members feel motivated and connected to the outcome.
- Share post-launch results, wins, and learnings with the entire team — celebrate together.

### Part 3: Skills by PM Career Level

Based on a framework by Ravi Mehta (senior product leader), the skills that matter most shift significantly as you progress.

| Career Level | Primary Focus |
|---|---|
| **APM / PM** | Execution, product craft, hard skills (problem solving, data, prioritization) |
| **Senior PM** | Product strategy, product discovery, working more independently |
| **Group PM** | Product vision, people management, cross-team strategy, hiring |
| **Director / VP** | Organizational design, building PM teams and practices |
| **CPO** | Company-level product vision, culture, executive alignment |

**Key insight:** At APM/PM level, you are typically told *what to do* by a senior PM and expected to execute well. From Senior PM onwards, you begin owning the discovery and strategy. From Group PM onwards, your value is in **scaling yourself through others** — managing PMs, setting direction, building systems that outlast individual contributors.

## 💡 Key Insights

1. **Fall in love with the problem, not the solution.** The bigger the problem, the more valuable the PM who can navigate it.
2. **A PM is the customer's advocate inside the organization.** When business interest conflicts with user experience, the PM holds the customer's voice.
3. **Product management is fundamentally risk management.** Ship the right thing, not just things that ship.
4. **Soft skills compound over time.** Hard skills get you in the door; soft skills determine how far you go.
5. **Lead with influence, not authority.** You rarely have direct reports — you must earn trust through transparency, credit-giving, and shielding your team.
6. **The ability to say no is a skill.** Learn to decline gracefully, with data, while keeping requesters informed and in the loop.
7. **Writing is a superpower.** Amazon's written culture demonstrates how structured writing replaces meetings and improves decision quality.
8. **Ask for help.** Especially as a new PM, expecting to move in silos is a mistake. Your senior PM or director is there to support you.

## 📚 Concepts and Terminology

- **First-Principles Thinking:** Deconstructing a problem to its fundamental truths and reasoning upward from there, rather than reasoning by analogy.
- **Product Portfolio:** A curated collection of case studies, product teardowns, wireframes, and roadmaps that demonstrates a PM's craft — essential for job seekers.
- **T-Shaped Profile:** A skills profile with deep expertise in 1–2 domains (the vertical bar of the T) and broad familiarity across many other relevant areas (the horizontal bar).
- **North Star Metric:** The single metric that best captures the core value your product delivers to customers. All sub-metrics support movement toward the North Star.
- **RICE Framework:** A prioritization scoring model using Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort.
- **MoSCoW Method:** Prioritization framework categorizing features as Must-have, Should-have, Could-have, and Won't-have.
- **OKR (Objectives and Key Results):** A goal-setting framework where product roadmaps are tied to measurable outcomes rather than output lists.
- **Continuous Discovery:** A product practice (from Teresa Torres) of running ongoing, lightweight customer research throughout the product lifecycle rather than in one-off research phases.
- **Scope Creep:** The gradual addition of unplanned features or requirements that expand beyond the original project scope, threatening timelines and team focus.
- **APM (Associate Product Manager):** Entry-level PM role, typically focused on execution and product craft under the guidance of a senior PM.
- **Group PM:** A senior PM role where the individual manages other product managers while also contributing individually to key products.
- **Value / Usability / Feasibility / Viability Risks:** The four core product risks that Teresa Torres and others use to frame product discovery and decision-making.

## 📌 Conclusion

Sahil Seni's talk is a comprehensive blueprint for PM skill development at every stage of a career. The central message is that **being a product manager means being a constant learner**: you must develop analytical depth, design empathy, technical literacy, business acumen, and interpersonal influence in parallel.

Crucially, the skills that dominate at the junior level (execution, product craft) give way to more strategic and people-oriented skills at senior levels (storytelling, stakeholder management, vision-setting, team building). Those who understand this progression — and deliberately invest in both hard and soft skills — are the ones who advance from APM to CPO.

For aspiring PMs: focus on building your product portfolio, mastering the basics of discovery, and asking for help. For experienced PMs: invest in writing, communication, stakeholder management, and the art of saying no with grace.
